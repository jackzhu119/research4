# Tracing absorption information in terahertz discrimination of medicinal materials

Ziyuan Zhu*

College of Artificial Intelligence Medicine, Chongqing Medical University, Chongqing, China

*Corresponding author: Ziyuan Zhu; zzy2630816871@gmail.com

## Abstract

Terahertz screening of medicinal materials benefits from identifying which spectral information supports a prediction and how additional labelled specimens change it. We investigated paired absorption and refractive-index spectra from 1,672 specimens representing four investigator-labelled materials. A common absorption classifier correctly classified 174/174 Suanzaoren–Zaoren prediction specimens and 331/339 specimens across all four materials. Representation analysis used 300 Suanzaoren specimens from five balanced source groups with repeated nested cross-validation. Monte Carlo partial-least-squares coefficient ranking followed by support vector classification achieved 93.44% mean accuracy with internally selected absorption features. At a fixed budget of 62 predictors, selection from a joint SNV absorption–refractive-index pool achieved 87.11%, compared with 81.00% for SNV absorption-only selection. All 930 retained feature occurrences across the 15 joint-pool refits were nevertheless absorption coordinates, revealing a candidate-pool effect on the selected absorption subsets. Separate support-set controls showed that adding valid labels increased five-source accuracy from 80.00% to 83.78% while retaining source-fitted standardization and kernel scale; unlabelled-statistics and loss-mass controls identified effective supervision as the principal explanation. Two accepted isolated-molecule calculations for protocatechuic acid provided 45 positive vibrational modes and independent infrared intensities, with lowest frequencies above the experimental modelling window. Together, these results connect medicinal-material discrimination to the variables and supervision actually used, supporting compact terahertz fingerprints with explicitly defined spectral and molecular interpretation.

Keywords: Terahertz spectroscopy; Medicinal materials; Suanzaoren; Feature selection; Spectral data fusion; Chemometrics

## Introduction

Discrimination of medicinal materials supports the interpretation of their quality and source characteristics. Suanzaoren is a useful example because visually similar commercial seeds can differ in material identity and chemical profile. The Hong Kong Chinese Materia Medica Standards monograph for Ziziphi Spinosae Semen includes microscopic and chromatographic identification, a chemical fingerprint and constituent assays.[1] These established methods provide a reference for complementary screening approaches. Here, Suanzaoren, Zaoren, Huangqi and Tianma denote the investigator-confirmed Chinese commercial material labels. A spectroscopic classifier of these labels answers a defined discrimination question; its application can be assessed alongside established identity and composition measurements.

Spectral fingerprints offer a practical route for this complementary task. Li et al. combined Fourier-transform near-infrared measurements and multivariate methods to evaluate Suanzaoren and adulterant mixtures.[2] Terahertz time-domain spectroscopy supplies both absorption and refractive-index information from a sample response. In an herbal application, Kolodochka et al. investigated geographical-group classification of Gastrodia roots using terahertz absorption variables and a principal-component representation.[3] These studies motivate a question beyond obtaining a high classification percentage: which information in a spectral workflow produces the observed discrimination, and what is gained by combining representations or obtaining further labels?

Absorption and refractive index provide paired descriptions of the same specimens. Their concatenation is a conventional form of low-level data fusion.[4] Once variable selection follows concatenation, however, two matrices become relevant: the complete candidate matrix used to estimate variable importance and the retained matrix received by the classifier. A second signal block can influence a multivariate ranking even when its variables do not survive selection. Matching the final predictor count therefore provides an important control, while tracing retained modality membership identifies the information actually supplied to the final model. This distinction makes a claim about spectral complementarity experimentally testable.

Variable-selection and validation methods supply the tools for that analysis. Monte Carlo coefficient ranking can reduce a large spectral matrix to a defined feature budget,[5] and nested validation separates model construction from the observations used for each performance estimate.[6,7] Repeated partitions expose dependence on the particular training subset. Recording selected coordinates adds a second dimension to the assessment: methods with similar accuracy may rely on different feature sets, while methods with different candidate pools may ultimately predict from the same modality.[8] The useful output is a spectral representation whose predictive role can be examined directly.

Additional labels introduce a related interpretive problem. A small labelled support set may be used to adjust classifier scores, update preprocessing statistics or refit a decision boundary. These operations change different parts of the prediction pipeline. Their effects are especially important when a reported adaptation gain is interpreted as correction of a physical measurement shift. Controlled comparisons can distinguish the contribution of valid supervision from changes in standardization, kernel scale and regularization. Such an explanation helps choose an adaptation strategy appropriate to the available labels.

Molecular calculations address a separate level of interpretation. An accepted vibrational model can supply specific frequencies and infrared intensities, while selected spectral coordinates identify empirical discrimination within a material collection. Linking the two requires agreement in frequency range and physical model. A local isolated-molecule calculation and a heterogeneous medicinal-material pellet represent different systems. Establishing what a calculation actually resolves provides a useful physical reference for interpreting terahertz fingerprints without assigning a constituent from classification alone.[9]

This study brings these questions together in a representation-focused terahertz workflow. A common absorption classifier establishes the application across four materials and their declared source groups. A balanced Suanzaoren population supports matched-budget comparisons of absorption and refractive index, including inspection of the features retained from joint pools. Controlled support-set experiments identify the operations responsible for additional-label gains. Qualified protocatechuic-acid calculations then provide a molecular frequency-scale reference. The resulting contribution is an explicit connection between the spectral candidates, final predictors and supervision underlying medicinal-material discrimination.

## Experimental and computational methods

### Materials and optical acquisition

The collection contained 18 workbooks and 1,672 specimen records: Suanzaoren, 500; Zaoren, 364; Huangqi, 403; and Tianma, 405 (Table I). Commercial products were purchased online with the declared source labels confirmed by the investigator. Separate investigator declarations covered specimen independence for the original 855 records and the additional 817 records. The five Suanzaoren groups were Inner Mongolia, Lingshi in Shanxi, Bozhou in Anhui, Shijiazhuang in Hebei and a separately labelled Shanxi group. Each comprised 100 specimens from one declared source batch. The two Shanxi labels were preserved as distinct recorded groups. Throughout this article, source discrimination refers to these declared groups.

The four materials shared an acquisition framework. Preparation equipment comprised an XQM-0.4L planetary ball mill, a DHG101-0B drying oven, an FA2104N electronic balance and an Atlas TM 15T manual press. Transmission measurements were made in the terahertz laboratory of the National Engineering Research Center for Information Technology in Agriculture using a TERA K15 system (Menlo Systems, Germany). The reported optical wavelength and optical power were 1,550 nm and 33 mW, respectively. Measurements were performed at 292 K in a nitrogen-purged enclosure with humidity below 4%. A blank polyethylene pellet supplied the reference response, followed by acquisition of the sample response. Polyethylene-reference response correction was confirmed by the investigator.

The time-domain workbook headers contain nominal thickness entries: 1.5 mm for 1,670 specimens and 1 mm for two Zaoren specimens from Yunnan. These are header-recorded nominal values. The analysis used the existing exported optical parameters. The exact available preparation and acquisition records, together with parameters requiring completion from the original laboratory record, are provided in Supplementary Section S1. These include pellet composition and pressing conditions, individual thickness measurements, correction software and settings, averaging and time-window settings, and the absorption-export unit.

### Optical parameters and paired representations

Four sheets within each workbook contain time-domain, frequency-domain, refractive-index and absorption-coefficient views of the same specimens. Sample columns were linked by identifier, and reference columns were excluded. The four views were counted once per specimen. Absorption and refractive index are denoted α and n. The processing window was 0.1–1.5 THz with 612 paired coordinates. The frequency convention follows the frozen processing protocol; the raw absorption-export unit remains unspecified, so plotted α values retain their exported scale.

Raw and standard-normal-variate (SNV) spectra were compared. SNV subtracts the mean of each spectrum and divides by its sample standard deviation. ### Analytical populations and common classification baseline

The frozen specimen roles were TRAIN 999, CAL 334 and PRED 339. Their material-specific counts appear in Table I; all 18 workbook splits and record identifiers are supplied in the supplementary evidence. Six classification tasks used the appropriate material subsets: Suanzaoren–Zaoren, four materials, and within-material source tasks for each of the four materials. Task roles were disjoint within each evaluation. The same specimens contribute to several tasks, so these tasks provide different prediction targets within one collection.

A common full-window baseline used 612 absorption coordinates, SNV, TRAIN-fitted standardization and an RBF support vector machine (SVM) with C = 10, gamma determined by the training-data scale rule, balanced class weights and seed 42. Each task used its entire TRAIN subset for fitting and its frozen PRED subset for evaluation; CAL was unused for this common baseline. Accuracy, macro-F1 and balanced accuracy were calculated from saved predictions. A training-majority reference and class-wise recalls were retained to contextualize unequal source sizes. In particular, three added Zaoren groups contain three specimens each, with one assigned to each role.

The principal representation analysis used the 300 Suanzaoren TRAIN specimens, comprising 60 specimens from each of five sources. Five stratified outer folds were repeated with seeds 42, 20261031 and 20261108. Each outer fit used 240 training and 60 evaluation specimens. Two inner folds selected the feature count and eligible classifier settings by mean macro-F1, with balanced accuracy, accuracy and archived deterministic simplicity rules resolving ties. All training-population transformations, feature ranking and model selection were fitted within the appropriate training fold.[6,7] Each repetition pooled its five outer-fold predictions over 300 specimens. Reported means average these three pooled estimates; they do not represent 900 independent specimens.

### Monte Carlo variable ranking and matched feature budgets

Variable ranking adapted Monte Carlo partial-least-squares coefficient stability from the MC-UVE and UVE families.[10,11] Each draw sampled a nominal fraction of 0.8 within each class without replacement, rounding each class-specific count upward. One-versus-rest partial-least-squares models with two components supplied coefficients. For each variable, the importance score combined the absolute mean-to-standard-deviation coefficient ratios across classes using their Euclidean norm, with a numerical floor protecting zero denominators. The variables with the K highest scores were retained. Because PLS coefficients depend on the full candidate matrix,[12] this procedure permits candidate-pool composition to influence the resulting ranking.

The base selector used 20 Monte Carlo draws (MC20). Controls used 100 draws (MC100) or averaged normalized ranks from five independent MC20 streams. Single-modality candidates were raw α, SNV α, raw n and SNV n. Joint candidates were SNV n plus SNV α, or raw n plus SNV α. Further controls retained approximately half of K from each block or applied an inner-selected weight to the n block. Joint selection ranked 1,224 candidates, and single-modality selection ranked 612. K always denotes the total final predictor count.

An RBF SVM followed selection, with C in {0.1, 1, 10, 100} and gamma equal to the training scale rule, one tenth of that rule or ten times that rule. Candidate budgets were K = 31, 62, 123, 184 and 245. Full-absorption shrinkage linear discriminant analysis (LDA) and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) supplied additional reference pipelines. Their complete archived settings are included in Supplementary Section S3. The primary comparison selected K in the inner folds. Fixed-K curves used the same outer partitions, while SVM settings remained inner-selected; they quantify sensitivity to a prescribed feature budget.

Saved outer-refit indices identified retained modality membership and frequency coordinate. We counted feature occurrences and calculated intersection size and Jaccard overlap between absorption subsets. Feature occurrences across refits are neither unique frequencies nor independent observations. For the newly recalculated fixed-K paired intervals, specimen identifiers were resampled as clusters, retaining all three paired predictions for each selected identifier. The archived primary-comparison intervals used 500 bootstrap draws; the separately reproduced fixed-K intervals used 5,000 draws with seed 20261109. These exploratory percentile intervals are conditional on the existing specimens and fits and are not adjusted for comparison across budgets.

### Support-label and decision-update controls

An additional mechanism study used Suanzaoren TRAIN specimens to construct 15 internal Source/Support/Query contexts under the three partition seeds and five folds. Each context contained 190 Source specimens, a 50-specimen Support pool and 60 Query specimens, corresponding to 38/10/12 per class. Source, Support and Query were disjoint within context. The source pipeline used the fixed full-absorption SNV/StandardScaler/RBF-SVM recipe. K-shot experiments supplied 1, 3, 5 or 10 labelled Support specimens per class, giving total support-label budgets of 5, 15, 25 or 50.

Comparisons included a frozen source classifier, score-anchor calibration, bias adjustment, conservative score blending and complete pipeline refitting. Mechanism controls refitted the SVM while freezing or re-estimating the Source scaler and gamma, updated unlabelled Support statistics, applied cyclically incorrect Support labels, or normalized total SVM loss mass. The latter adjusted C so that C multiplied by the number of training specimens matched the source-only value. A separate acquisition comparison used total budgets of 5, 15, 25 or 50 labels, with uncertainty, diversity and three random acquisition sequences. This total-budget experiment does not guarantee an equal number of labels in each class. Exact designs and all positive and negative results are retained in Supplementary Section S7 and the accompanying tables.

The original corrected matrix, the additional representation matrix and the five mechanism rounds comprise different computational comparisons on overlapping specimens. Continuations and backups were counted once. The evaluations are retrospective internal specimen-level studies on a previously developed collection. An independently acquired support/query batch study was not performed. Uncertainty intervals cluster repeated predictions by physical specimen and describe this internal collection; they do not estimate between-batch variation.

### Molecular frequency and infrared calculations

Protocatechuic acid was evaluated as an isolated neutral molecule using PBE/def2-SVP in PySCF.[13–15] Two accepted numerical realizations were considered: direct Coulomb treatment with integration grid level 5 and RI-J density fitting with grid level 5. Each realization had its own accepted stationary-point and mode evidence and independently evaluated dipole derivatives for infrared intensities. A nonlinear 17-atom molecule has 45 vibrational modes. We used the complete accepted 45-mode tables and the sealed numerical acceptance records, rather than selecting individual frequencies from incomplete jobs. Frequencies were converted using 1 cm⁻¹ = 0.0299792458 THz. The scope is a local isolated-molecule harmonic model; no solid-state spectrum or constituent concentration was inferred. Numerical differentiation settings and the complete frequency–intensity tables appear in Supplementary Section S8.

## Results and discussion

### A common absorption classifier across materials and sources

The common absorption baseline correctly classified all 174 Suanzaoren–Zaoren PRED specimens and 331/339 specimens across four materials (Table II). The four-material macro-F1 was 0.9751 and balanced accuracy was 97.50%. These results establish useful separation of the recorded materials with a single preprocessing and classifier recipe. Huangqi and Tianma therefore contribute direct application cases within the study, alongside the seed-material comparison.

The same recipe produced source-group accuracies of 85/100 for Suanzaoren, 69/82 for Huangqi and 81/83 for Tianma. These tasks differ in class structure and represented source composition. Their results describe the available collections rather than a sequence of performance improvements. Source-group classification also has a different target from material discrimination: the former evaluates variation among specimens assigned to the same material, whereas the latter can use differences between material categories.

Zaoren illustrates the importance of class-balanced reporting. Its 72/74 accuracy corresponded to macro-F1 0.5972 and balanced accuracy 60.00%. Yan’an supplied 70 of the 74 PRED specimens, and a training-majority classifier would correctly classify 70/74. Two sparsely represented sources had zero recall. The full six-task table therefore retains these source-level measures while the balanced Suanzaoren task supplies the principal population for representation analysis. This allocation gives the broad application results and detailed spectral comparisons distinct analytical roles.

### Absorption supplies the strongest internally selected representations

Within the balanced five-source Suanzaoren task, SNV absorption with MC20–SVM achieved 93.44% mean nested-validation accuracy and macro-F1 0.9344 (Table III). The mean retained count was 220.6 of the 612 absorption coordinates. Raw-absorption MC20 achieved 93.22%, and MC100 with SNV absorption achieved the same mean accuracy as MC20. Thus, the central result is a discriminative absorption representation with an explicit feature budget; the 0.22-percentage-point SNV–raw difference does not establish a distinct accuracy advance.

The modality comparison provides a clearer spectroscopic distinction. Raw refractive index achieved 83.67%, while SNV refractive index achieved 62.56%. In contrast, both raw and SNV absorption retained approximately 93% accuracy. Removing a spectrum’s overall level and scale therefore affected the two exported representations differently. This observation identifies a representation-dependent contribution of spectral level and shape within the collection. It does not isolate a particular chemical or preparation factor responsible for that contribution.

The internally selected joint SNV pool achieved 91.67%, and the weighted and equal-block controls achieved 89.22% and 88.78%, respectively. The full set of representation results is retained in Table III, allowing the feature-budget findings to be interpreted alongside the primary selection procedure. Increasing Monte Carlo draws from 20 to 100 produced five additional correct and five additional incorrect repeated predictions relative to MC20. More coefficient draws therefore did not produce a corresponding gain in mean accuracy. The five-stream rank-aggregation control achieved 92.78%; averaging rankings and improving prediction are separate empirical outcomes.

### Joint candidate pools change the selected absorption information

The fixed-budget comparison revealed a result that the primary accuracy table alone does not explain (Fig. 2). At K = 62, selection from the joint SNV n + SNV α pool achieved 87.11%, compared with 81.00% for SNV α alone. Both final classifiers received 62 predictors, approximately one tenth of the single absorption grid. The paired mean difference was 6.11 percentage points, with a reproduced 5,000-draw specimen-cluster interval of 2.33–10.00 percentage points. Raw-absorption selection at the same budget achieved 88.78%, establishing that the joint-pool gain is specific to the matched SNV comparison. All five budget comparisons appear in the figure and Supplementary Table S3. The internally selected K result remains the primary performance estimate; K = 62 is a prescribed-budget comparison.

Inspection of the retained coordinates identified the information pathway. Every one of the 15 joint-pool outer refits at K = 62 selected 62 absorption variables and zero refractive-index variables. Across those refits, all 930 feature occurrences were absorption coordinates (Fig. 3). Consequently, the restricted-budget gain can be obtained by a final absorption-only predictor matrix even when refractive-index candidates participated in ranking. This is a concrete distinction between integrating candidate information and retaining both modalities in prediction.

The two selection procedures also chose substantially different absorption subsets, with a mean Jaccard overlap of 0.0111 at K = 62. Equal predictor width and identical final modality therefore do not imply an identical spectral representation. PLS coefficient-stability ranking is conditioned on the complete candidate covariance structure. Adding a signal block can change that ranking and the absorption coordinates retained by the final SVM. The observed result supports this candidate-pool interpretation; it does not by itself identify which individual added variable caused a ranking change or separate subset effects from the independently inner-selected SVM settings.

The practical implication is to document three quantities when evaluating selected spectral fusion: the candidate blocks supplied to the selector, the total retained budget and the modality composition of the final predictors. Reporting accuracy and K alone would miss the absorption-only endpoint. Conversely, inspecting retained modality without a paired matched-budget comparison would miss the change in predictive behavior. Their combination explains how a compact spectral representation arose and prevents the candidate pool from being treated as evidence that both modalities contributed directly to prediction.

### Additional-label gains arise primarily from effective supervision

The Source/Support/Query controls identify a second information pathway (Table IV; Fig. 4). The frozen five-source classifier achieved 80.00% accuracy with 190 Source specimens in each context. Adding 10 true Support labels per class and refitting the full pipeline increased accuracy to 83.89%. Across 900 correlated Query predictions, refitting corrected 49 source-model errors and introduced 14 errors, giving a net increase of 35 correct predictions. This comparison concerns additional supervision within the 300-specimen TRAIN population.

Most of that gain remained when the Source scaler and kernel gamma were frozen: refitting with the valid Support labels achieved 83.78%, a paired increase of 3.78 percentage points with a descriptive specimen-cluster 95% interval of 2.11–5.67 percentage points. Re-estimating the scaler and gamma yielded 83.89%, close to the frozen-transform result. The decomposition therefore locates the main contribution in the updated supervised decision function. It supplies a direct explanation for the label-budget effect without assigning it to a change in measurement conditions.

Two further controls sharpen that interpretation. Updating unlabelled Support statistics gave 80.11%, while refitting with cyclically incorrect Support labels gave 73.33%. Valid labels supplied information that the unlabelled-statistics update did not reproduce. In the loss-mass control, Source-plus-Support refitting achieved 83.78% both with C fixed at 10 and with C reduced to 7.9167 to preserve the source-only total C × N. Increasing source-only loss mass retained 80.00%. Under this design, the additional supervised specimens explain the benefit more directly than an increase in aggregate penalty weight.

Score adjustment produced a different pattern. At 10 labels per class, the strong anchor, bias-only adjustment and weak blend achieved 78.78%, 79.78% and 80.11%, respectively. The weak blend changed all 900 five-source Query score vectors but changed two predicted labels; 893 predictions satisfied the recorded strict-margin preservation certificate. Numerical score motion therefore need not imply meaningful boundary adaptation. These results define a useful operating distinction: when valid additional labels are available, updating the decision function is supported by the present evidence; a small score correction can mostly preserve the existing classifier.

The acquisition experiment further examined label efficiency. With a total budget of five acquired labels, uncertainty-guided full refitting achieved 81.67%, compared with 80.00–80.22% across three random acquisition sequences and 80.33% for diversity acquisition. At a total budget of 50, all acquisition strategies used the entire Support pool and converged to 83.78%. This pattern makes acquisition order relevant at restricted budgets. Its dependence on the subsequent learner is also visible: the uncertainty sequence with a guarded mean-anchor update achieved 78.11% at budget 15, while full refitting achieved 82.67%. Complete acquisition and calibration results appear in the supplement.

Auxiliary-material pretraining was similarly conditional. In the fixed three-seed transfer comparison, target-only, additional-target-pretraining and auxiliary-material-pretraining arms averaged 81.00%, 80.33% and 79.00%, respectively. Their target data and representation budgets are reported separately from the Source/Support/Query experiment. This control supports selecting an adaptation operation on its matched target evidence rather than treating extra pretraining as an automatic gain. The complete corrected neural and classical matrix, including negative results, is preserved in the supplementary data.

### Qualified molecular calculations define a useful frequency reference

The accepted protocatechuic-acid results add a specific computational result to the spectral analysis. Both direct-grid5 and RI-J-grid5 PBE/def2-SVP realizations produced 45 positive vibrational modes with independently evaluated infrared intensities. Their lowest frequencies were 74.0312 and 74.0062 cm⁻¹, equivalent to 2.21940 and 2.21865 THz. The corresponding lowest-mode intensities were 2.14307 and 2.13888 km mol⁻¹. Sorted-mode frequency differences between the two realizations were at most 0.404864 cm⁻¹, providing a numerical agreement check for this local model (Supplementary Fig. S1).

No calculated protocatechuic-acid fundamental from either accepted realization falls within 0.1–1.5 THz. This gives the molecular calculation a precise role: it constrains an isolated-molecule explanation of the modelling window. The selected experimental absorption coordinates cannot be assigned to an isolated protocatechuic-acid harmonic fundamental on the basis of these calculations. They remain empirical features of the measured materials. Establishing a molecular assignment in that window would require a physical model appropriate to the sample environment and direct supporting measurements.

The qualification is molecule- and model-specific. Complete accepted Hessian and infrared results were not available for calycosin and spinosin at the evidence cutoff. Their computational progress is recorded in the supplementary source register, rather than used to assign experimental peaks. The accepted protocatechuic-acid calculation thus strengthens the article through a reproducible frequency-scale reference while keeping the principal spectroscopic claim connected to the measured absorption predictors.

## Conclusions

Paired terahertz representations supported complementary discrimination of four recorded medicinal materials and detailed analysis of balanced Suanzaoren source groups. Internally selected absorption features achieved 93.44% mean accuracy. At a restricted 62-feature budget, joint-SNV candidate selection exceeded SNV-absorption selection while retaining absorption variables exclusively, exposing a different absorption-only endpoint at matched predictor width. Support-set controls identified valid additional supervision and decision-function updating as the principal source of the observed label-budget gain. Accepted protocatechuic-acid frequency and infrared calculations supplied a defined molecular reference above the modelling window. Together, these findings establish a traceable path from spectral candidates and additional labels to the final predictions, giving terahertz medicinal-material screening an explicit basis for representation design and interpretation.
